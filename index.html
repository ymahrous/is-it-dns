What is Is It DNS?

Is It DNS? is a free, interactive troubleshooting flowchart for engineers on call. You pick a symptom, answer a few questions, and it concludes that the cause is DNS, every time. Each of its 22 paths ends in a real DNS failure, with the dig output that proves it, the root cause and the fix.

How do I check if it's DNS?

Five checks, in order. Most DNS problems show up by step three.

  1. Resolve the nameRun dig +short example.com. No answer, or the wrong IP, means DNS.
  2. Connect by IPRun curl -H "Host: example.com" http://203.0.113.10 with the server's real IP. If that works and the hostname doesn't, it's DNS.
  3. Compare resolversQuery dig @1.1.1.1, dig @8.8.8.8 and your internal resolver. Different answers point to caching or split-horizon DNS.
  4. Ask the authoritative serverRun dig +trace example.com. If the authoritative answer is right but others are wrong, wait out the old TTL.
  5. Check the machine itselfRead the hosts file and /etc/resolv.conf on the machine that's failing.

12 ways it's DNS

Every ending in the flowchart is one of these. Each is a real failure you'll meet on call.

stale A record

Why does my site fail by hostname but work by IP?

The hostname's A or AAAA record points at the wrong address, often an old server or load balancer left behind after a migration. Run dig +short example.com A and compare the answer with the server's real IP. Update the record, then wait for the old TTL to expire.

Source: RFC 1035
TTL

Why do some users see the new site and others the old one?

Resolvers cache each record for its TTL (time to live), in seconds. With a TTL of 86400, a resolver can keep serving the old answer for up to 24 hours after you change it. Lower the TTL to 300 a day or two before any planned change.

Source: RFC 1035 §3.2.1
zone apex

Why does www work but the bare domain doesn't?

The bare domain, called the zone apex, can't hold a CNAME, because a CNAME can't coexist with the SOA and NS records every apex has. Add an A or AAAA record at the apex, or use your provider's ALIAS, ANAME or CNAME-flattening feature.

Source: RFC 1034 §3.6.2
split-horizon

Why do health checks fail when the site works for users?

With split-horizon DNS, internal and public resolvers return different answers for the same name. If the internal zone still lists an old private IP, anything inside the network, like a health checker, fails. Compare dig results from your internal resolver and from 1.1.1.1.

SPF

Why does email fail SPF when the record looks right?

SPF allows at most 10 DNS lookups per check. Every include:, a, mx, exists and redirect counts, including nested ones. Past 10, receivers return permerror and many treat the mail as failing. Remove unused senders or flatten the record.

Source: RFC 7208 §4.6.4
MX

Why are emails bouncing with “host not found”?

Usually the domain has no MX record, or the MX target has no A or AAAA record, or the target is a CNAME, which RFC 2181 forbids. Check with dig +short example.com MX, then look up each target name it returns.

Source: RFC 2181 §10.3
CoreDNS

Why can't my Kubernetes pods resolve service names?

Pods send DNS queries to CoreDNS through the kube-dns service. If the CoreDNS pods are crash-looping or out of memory, every lookup in the cluster fails or times out. Check kubectl -n kube-system get pods -l k8s-app=kube-dns and raise memory limits or replicas.

Source: Kubernetes: Debugging DNS Resolution
ndots

Why are external API calls from Kubernetes slow?

Kubernetes sets ndots:5 by default, so a name with fewer than five dots is tried against every search domain first. One call to api.example.com can become several failed queries before the real one. Add a trailing dot or lower ndots in the pod's dnsConfig.

Source: Kubernetes: DNS for Services and Pods
GeoDNS

Why is the site down in only one region?

GeoDNS returns different answers depending on where the query comes from. A stale geo rule can send a whole region to a server that no longer exists. Test with dig +subnet= and a client range against your authoritative server, or query from probes in that region.

Source: RFC 7871
negative caching

Why is the site still down for one person after the fix?

Resolvers cache failures too. After an NXDOMAIN, a resolver keeps the negative answer for the lower of the SOA record's minimum field and its TTL. A home router or laptop can keep saying the name doesn't exist until that expires. Flushing the local DNS cache fixes it.

Source: RFC 2308 §5
hosts file

Why does it work on my machine and nowhere else?

Your computer checks its hosts file (/etc/hosts, or C:\Windows\System32\drivers\etc\hosts) before asking DNS. An old entry there overrides the real record for you alone. dig skips the hosts file, so compare it with ping or curl to spot the difference.

resolv.conf

Why does DNS fail on one server when it works everywhere else?

The server's /etc/resolv.conf decides which resolver it asks. If it points at 127.0.0.53 while systemd-resolved is stopped, or at a resolver that no longer exists, every lookup fails. Read the file, then query the listed nameserver directly with dig @address.

Questions

What does “it's always DNS” mean?

It's a long-running joke among sysadmins and SREs: when something breaks, the cause is DNS far more often than anyone expects. DNS sits under almost every connection, caches answers for hours, and small record mistakes fail in confusing ways. This flowchart turns the joke into a diagnosis that ends in DNS every time.

How long does DNS propagation take?

DNS changes don't really propagate. Cached copies expire. Each resolver keeps the old record until the TTL it had when cached runs out, so the longest wait is the old TTL: 5 minutes for 300 seconds, a full day for 86400. Negative answers follow the SOA minimum instead.

Is this a real troubleshooting tool?

Partly. Every path ends in DNS on purpose, so it's a joke first. But each ending describes a real, common DNS failure, with the command that confirms it, the fix, and the RFC or documentation behind it. The five-step check on this page is a sound first pass in a real incident.
